How to decide a wedding guest list — the cut rules that actually hold

The guest list is the one decision that sets every other number on the budget. Here is a way to build it that produces a defensible list instead of an argument, and the four cut rules that survive contact with real families.

Free to read · last reviewed 2026-09-18

Start from the constraint, not the names

Almost every painful guest list starts the same way: someone opens a blank document and begins typing names. The list grows to whatever length affection produces, and then it has to be cut back to whatever the venue and the money allow — so every cut feels like a rejection of a person rather than a consequence of a room size.

Reverse it. Settle two numbers first, before a single name is written down.

  1. The capacity number. What the venues you would realistically book actually hold, seated, with whatever dance floor or staging you want. Seated capacity is usually well below the number in the brochure.
  2. The per-head number. Catering, drinks, service, rentals and the stationery and favours that scale per person. This is the figure that turns one extra name into a real cost.

Divide the money you are willing to spend on per-head items by that per-head number. That quotient, capped by seated capacity, is your guest count. It was decided by arithmetic, which is the entire point — it means the count is not something anyone has to win.

Build it in tiers, once

Write the list in three passes and do not re-order it afterwards. Each pass is a different question, and keeping them separate is what stops the list becoming a single undifferentiated blob of 190 names.

  • Tier A — the day is diminished without them. Immediate family, the people who would be in the photographs on the mantelpiece in thirty years. If you are hesitating, it is not Tier A.
  • Tier B — you would be glad to see them there. Extended family you are genuinely close to, long-standing friends, the people you would invite to a significant birthday.
  • Tier C — you would invite them if the room were bigger. Colleagues, wider family, friends you see once a year, plus-ones of single guests.

Now compare Tier A plus Tier B against the count you calculated. If it already exceeds the count, the problem is the venue or the budget, not the list — and that is a far more tractable conversation than deciding between two cousins.

The four cut rules

Cuts cause arguments when they look arbitrary. They stop causing arguments when they are rules applied to everyone, because then the answer to “why wasn’t she invited?” is a rule rather than a judgement about her. Pick your rules before you apply them to any specific name.

1. The time rule

If you have not had a real conversation with them in a defined window — many couples use two years — they are not on the list. Applied consistently, this removes a surprising number of names without anyone being singled out.

2. The symmetry rule

Whatever you do for one category, do for the equivalent category on the other side. If one set of cousins is invited, all cousins of that degree are. If work colleagues are out, they are out for both of you. Asymmetry is what families actually notice and remember.

3. The plus-one rule

Set one condition and apply it to every single guest without exception — commonly living together, engaged, or together beyond some length of time. “We know their partner and not theirs” is the rule that generates the most lasting resentment, because from the outside it is indistinguishable from favouritism.

4. The children rule

All, none, or immediate family only. There is no fourth option that holds. Whichever you pick, put it on the invitation by addressing it to named adults only, and tell the most affected families directly and early rather than letting them infer it from an envelope.

The parents' list

This is where most guest lists actually break, and it is rarely about the individual names. If parents are contributing financially, many expect a say, and the expectation is usually unspoken until it collides with a cut.

The mechanism that tends to work is an allocation rather than a veto: each set of parents gets a fixed number of seats from your Tier C budget and fills them however they like. It converts an open-ended negotiation over the whole list into a closed one over a number, and it hands them the cuts within their own allocation — which is both fairer and considerably quieter.

Agree the allocation before anyone sees a draft list. Once a name has been written down and seen, removing it costs far more than never having promised the seat.

Track it so the number stays honest

A guest list is not a list of names. It is at minimum: name, tier, household grouping, postal address, email, invitation sent, RSVP, meal choice, dietary requirement, and a plus-one flag. Households matter more than people for stationery and seating; the count matters for catering. Keeping both in one place is what stops the two drifting apart.

Expect declines, but do not plan around a specific rate. Local weddings with close family run very differently from destination weddings, and inviting beyond capacity on the assumption that enough people will decline is the version of this that occasionally goes badly wrong. If you want to use the tiers as a waiting list, do it deliberately: send Tier C invitations in a second wave, early enough that the timing is not obvious.

Common questions

How do you decide who to cut from a wedding guest list?
Decide the rule before you look at the name. Consistent rules — a contact-recency window, symmetry between both sides, one plus-one condition for everyone, and a single policy on children — mean each cut is the result of a policy rather than a judgement about that person, which is what makes it defensible when someone asks.
How many guests should you invite to a wedding?
It falls out of two numbers rather than being chosen: the seated capacity of venues you would realistically book, and your per-head cost for catering, drinks and anything else that scales per person. The budget you allocate to per-head items divided by that cost, capped by capacity, is the count.
Do you have to give everyone a plus-one?
No, but whatever condition you set has to apply to every single guest. Common conditions are living together, engaged, or together beyond a set length of time. Making exceptions for partners you happen to know is the choice most likely to be read as favouritism.
How do you handle parents adding names to the guest list?
Give each set of parents a fixed allocation of seats and let them fill it themselves, agreed before anyone sees a draft. It turns an open negotiation over your whole list into a closed one over a number, and it moves the difficult cuts inside their own allocation.
Should you invite more people than the venue holds and count on declines?
Doing it on an assumed decline rate is risky, because rates vary hugely with distance, season and how close the circle is. If you want the effect, do it deliberately as tiered waves — send a second round of invitations early enough that the timing is not conspicuous.
Planning guidance, not financial or legal advice. Per-head costs, venue rules and contract terms vary enormously by region, season and supplier — treat every figure here as a frame to be replaced with real quotes, and read your own venue and catering contracts before committing to any number of guests.
