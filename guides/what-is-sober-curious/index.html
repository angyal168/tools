What is sober curious — the term, and what people usually mean by it

Sober curious describes questioning the default rather than adopting a label. Here is where the phrase came from, what it usually does and does not refer to, and the practical problem it leaves behind: what to actually hold at the party.

Free to read · last reviewed 2026-09-18

Where the term comes from

The phrase was popularised by the writer Ruby Warrington, whose 2018 book Sober Curious gave a name to something a lot of people were already doing quietly: questioning whether they wanted a drink in a given moment, rather than assuming it.

The emphasis sits on the second word. It describes an inquiry, not an outcome. Someone sober curious is asking what drinking is doing for them in a particular setting — and the answer is allowed to vary by occasion.

What it usually describes

Used as most people use it, it tends to mean some combination of:

  • Treating a drink as a choice to be made each time rather than the automatic thing that happens at six o'clock or at a wedding.
  • Noticing what alcohol is actually contributing to an evening — and noticing the occasions where the honest answer is very little.
  • Drinking less, or sometimes not at all, without adopting an identity or ruling anything out permanently.
  • Still wanting the social object: something in a glass, made with some care, that signals you are participating.

That last point is doing more work than it looks like it is, and it is the one this page is really about.

What it is generally not used to mean

The term is usually distinguished from sobriety in the sense of recovery from alcohol dependence, which is a different thing with a different weight, its own communities, and clinical dimensions that a lifestyle phrase does not touch. Many people in recovery are explicit that the two should not be collapsed together, and the distinction is worth respecting simply as a matter of accuracy.

It is also not a claim about health outcomes. The word describes a posture toward a habit — nothing more — and this page makes no claim in either direction about what changing that habit does. Those are questions for a clinician who knows the person asking, not for an article.

The practical problem nobody warns you about

People who try it almost always report running into the same wall, and it is not willpower. It is that the non-alcoholic options in front of them are bad.

The standard offering is a soft drink, a juice, or a fruit purée with a sprig of something in it. All three share one defect: they are sweet, and sweetness is the only thing they are. A cocktail is not interesting because it contains alcohol — it is interesting because it is balanced, with bitterness and acidity pushing back against the sugar and aromatics giving it a top note. Remove the spirit and, in most zero-proof drinks, nothing takes over that structural job. What is left is cordial in a nicer glass.

So the evening becomes an exercise in holding something disappointing while other people hold something good, which is a much harder thing to sustain than the decision itself was.

What makes a zero-proof drink worth holding

The fix is structural rather than a matter of finding a substitute for the spirit. Build the glass out of the four things a good cocktail balances, and the absence stops being the point:

  • Bitter — the element most often missing entirely. Bitters, an infusion, strong tea, citrus pith, a bitter aperitif-style syrup. This is what stops a drink reading as sweet.
  • Sour — fresh citrus, or a shrub, which is fruit and sugar cut with vinegar. Acidity is what makes a drink refreshing rather than cloying.
  • Sweet — present to balance the other two, not to carry the drink. Far less than a purée-based mocktail uses.
  • Aromatic — herbs, spice, peel oils, a smoked or salted rim. Much of what we register as flavour arrives through the nose.

Texture and temperature matter more than they do with alcohol, too: something to give the liquid weight, proper ice, and a glass that suits it. A drink built this way is a drink, not a stand-in for one — which is the difference between an evening that works and one you are enduring.

Common questions

What does sober curious mean?
It describes treating each drink as an open question rather than an assumption — noticing what alcohol contributes on a given occasion and choosing accordingly, often drinking less as a result, without adopting a permanent label or ruling anything out.
Is sober curious the same as being sober?
It is generally used for something different. Sobriety in the sense of recovery from alcohol dependence carries clinical and personal weight that a lifestyle term does not, and many people in recovery are clear that the two should not be treated as the same thing.
Where did the term sober curious come from?
It was popularised by the writer Ruby Warrington, whose 2018 book of that name gave a name to a shift a lot of people were already making privately. The emphasis is on curiosity — an inquiry into a habit rather than a rule about it.
Why do most mocktails taste disappointing?
Because they are usually only sweet. What makes a cocktail interesting is balance — bitterness and acidity pushing against the sugar, with aromatics on top. Remove the spirit without replacing that structure and nothing is holding the sweetness in check.
What makes a good non-alcoholic drink?
The same proportions a good cocktail has: something bitter, something sour, restrained sweetness, and an aromatic element, plus attention to texture, ice and glass. Built that way the drink stands on its own rather than reading as a substitute for something missing.
