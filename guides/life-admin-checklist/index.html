A life admin checklist that survives a busy week

Most life admin lists fail for the same reason: they mix a five-minute task with a five-hour one and give you no way to tell which is which. This one is sorted by what it costs you to do, so a fifteen-minute gap is still usable.

Free to read · last reviewed 2026-08-14

Sort by time cost, not by category

The standard life admin list is grouped by area — money, health, home, legal. It reads well and it does not get done, because every group opens with something that needs an afternoon and a phone call during business hours. The first unfinishable item stops the list.

Sorting by time cost fixes the failure without changing the contents. You get three buckets:

  • Fifteen minutes — anything you can finish alone, at a desk, without waiting on another person.
  • An hour — needs gathering, comparing, or a single call.
  • Half a day — needs decisions, other people, or documents you do not currently have.

The practical effect is that the fifteen-minute bucket empties itself over a couple of weeks of small gaps, and clearing it removes about half of the background hum without ever needing a free afternoon.

The fifteen-minute bucket

Each of these is finishable in one sitting. None of them requires anyone else to be available.

  • Photograph both sides of every card in your wallet and store the images somewhere you can reach without the wallet.
  • Write down where the important documents physically are — not the documents, just the locations. This single list is what people wish existed during an emergency.
  • List your recurring subscriptions and their renewal dates from your bank or card statement, not from memory.
  • Check the expiry dates on passports, licences and any professional registrations. Renewals often need lead time you do not discover until it is short.
  • Name one emergency contact who is not in your household and tell them.
  • Set a calendar reminder for the next known deadline you are aware of and currently carrying in your head.

If you do nothing else on this page, the document-location list and the expiry check are the two with the worst downside when skipped.

The one-hour bucket

These need gathering or comparing, but they still finish in one sitting once you start.

  • A current medication and condition list for each household member — what is taken, why, and who prescribed it. Useful at every appointment and essential at an unplanned one.
  • A single view of insurance — which policies exist, what each covers, what each costs, when each renews. Most households discover a duplicate or a lapse the first time they write this down.
  • An account inventory — banks, pensions, brokerages, utilities. Names and locations only, not credentials in a document.
  • A household running-cost baseline — twelve months of fixed outgoings from statements. It takes an hour and it changes what the rest of the year's decisions are made against.
  • Beneficiary and named-contact fields on accounts and policies. These are frequently set once and never revisited after a life change.

The half-day bucket — and how to start it anyway

These are the items that never begin, because starting them feels like committing to finishing them. The way through is to split each into a first hour that produces something usable on its own.

  • Estate paperwork — the full job is long. The first hour is an inventory: what exists, who is named, where it is kept, what is out of date. That inventory is what any professional would ask for first anyway.
  • Comparing health cover — the first hour is building the comparison table with your actual usage in it, not choosing.
  • Medical records — the first hour is requesting them; they arrive on someone else's schedule, so requesting early is most of the win.
  • Registering a business entity — the first hour is deciding the structure question and checking name availability, which is what everything else waits on.
  • Enrolment deadlines for anything age- or date-triggered — the first hour is finding out what the real dates are for your situation, because a missed window can carry lasting cost.

None of these need to be finished the day they are started. They need to be started on a day that is not the day they become urgent.

The once-a-year pass

A short annual review catches the things that expire quietly. Put it on a fixed date rather than a rolling reminder — a date you can miss and still notice you missed.

  • Expiry dates: identity documents, professional registrations, warranties, domain names.
  • Anything that changed after a life event — a move, a birth, a marriage, a death, a job change. These are the highest-yield items on the page, because they are the ones most likely to be silently out of date.
  • Insurance renewals, which often auto-renew at a worse rate than the one available to a new customer.
  • Subscriptions you have not used in six months.
  • Whether the document-location list from the fifteen-minute bucket is still accurate.

The realistic standard is not a perfectly maintained system. It is that the next person who needs to find something — including you, in a bad week — can find it without a search.

Common questions

What is life admin?
The unpaid administrative work of running a household — paperwork, renewals, insurance, appointments, accounts, deadlines. It is unbounded by nature, which is why sorting it by time cost tends to work better than trying to complete it.
How often should a life admin checklist be reviewed?
A short annual pass catches most expiries, and any life event — a move, a birth, a job change, a bereavement — is worth its own small review, since those are what most often leave a document or a named contact out of date.
What is the single most useful item to do first?
For most households, writing down where the important documents physically live. It takes minutes, needs nobody else, and it is the thing people most often wish existed when something goes wrong.
Should passwords go in this list?
Credentials belong in a purpose-built password manager rather than in a document alongside account names. An inventory that says which accounts exist is useful; one that also carries the keys to them turns a single lost file into a much larger problem.
