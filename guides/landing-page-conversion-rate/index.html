How to improve landing page conversion rate

Most conversion advice assumes thousands of visitors and a statistics engine. Below a few hundred a month, A/B testing tells you nothing — and the fixes that work at that scale are different, blunter, and mostly free.

Free to read · last reviewed 2026-08-13

First, is it actually a conversion problem?

This is the question that saves the most wasted work, and it is answered with arithmetic rather than opinion.

A 2 percent conversion rate is roughly typical for cold traffic to a paid digital product. At that rate, 50 visitors a month produces an expected one sale — and one is close enough to zero that a month with none is unremarkable noise, not a signal about your page.

Below roughly 200 visitors a month, a page with zero sales tells you almost nothing about the page. It tells you about the traffic.

So before optimising anything, get the visitor count. If you cannot get it, that is the first thing to fix — a page you cannot measure cannot be improved on purpose, only by luck. And if the number is small, the honest conclusion is that distribution, not copy, is the binding constraint this month.

Why A/B testing does not apply at this scale

Detecting a change from 2 percent to 3 percent at conventional confidence needs on the order of a few thousand visitors per variant. At 200 visitors a month, that test concludes some time next decade.

Running it anyway is worse than not testing, because a split test on tiny numbers produces a winner almost every time — noise reliably favours one side — and you then act on it with the confidence a test is supposed to buy. The one thing you can conclude below the threshold is that you cannot conclude anything.

What replaces it: fix things that are defects on their own terms, whether or not a test could measure them. A page that does not state its price is worse than one that does, and you do not need a test to know it.

The diagnostic order

Run these in sequence and stop at the first one that fails. Later items are irrelevant if an earlier one is broken.

  1. Does the page load, on a phone, quickly? Most traffic is mobile. A page that reflows for three seconds loses people before any copy is read.
  2. Within five seconds, is it clear what this is and who it is for? Hand the URL to someone unfamiliar, give them five seconds, ask what it sells. If they cannot say, nothing below matters.
  3. Is the price visible without a click? A hidden price reads as an unpleasant surprise being deferred, and people leave rather than find out.
  4. Is there exactly one thing to do? Two competing calls to action reliably underperform one.
  5. Is the top objection answered on the page? Usually one of: will this work for my situation, what exactly do I receive, what happens if it is wrong for me.
  6. Does the buyer know what arrives after payment? "A 40-page PDF and two spreadsheets, instantly" converts better than "instant access."

The six defects that account for most of the loss

Consistent across small pages, and all fixable in an afternoon without a test:

  • The headline describes the product instead of the reader's problem. "Comprehensive planning toolkit" is about you. "Work out what your insurance actually covers before you need it" is about them.
  • No specificity about the deliverable. Counts, formats, page numbers, file types. Vagueness reads as evasion, especially at low price points where the buyer is deciding fast.
  • The call to action is a verb with no object. "Get started" versus "Download the checklist — $9." Restating the price at the button removes the last hesitation.
  • Nothing establishes why you. At low prices this need not be credentials; the specific detail only a practitioner would know does the same job faster than a bio.
  • The disqualifier is missing. Saying plainly who the product is not for raises trust and, counterintuitively, conversion — it signals the rest of the page is not overselling.
  • Friction at the end. Extra fields, account creation, an unexplained redirect. Every step after the decision loses a share of the people who had already decided.

Using an agent for this without getting slop

Asked to "improve conversion," a model returns a listicle of generic best practice, most of which does not apply and none of which is grounded in your page. Constrain it instead:

Fetch this URL and work only from what it actually serves.

1. In one sentence, what is sold and to whom? Quote the exact
   text you inferred it from.
2. List every claim on the page and mark each as substantiated
   on-page, or not.
3. List the three objections the page does NOT answer.
4. Quote the CTA verbatim and say what happens after the click,
   or say the page does not tell you.

Do not suggest improvements yet. Do not use knowledge about
this product that is not on the page.

Step 4 is the one that finds real defects, because "the page does not say" is a finding you can act on immediately. Ask for rewrites only after the diagnosis, and require each one to cite the line it replaces — otherwise you get a new page rather than a fixed one, and you lose the parts that were working.

The uncomfortable conclusion

If the page is clear, priced, specific, single-CTA and honest about who it is not for, and it still makes no sales at 40 visitors a month, the page is probably fine. Forty visitors is not a verdict.

At that point continuing to rewrite copy is the comfortable work — visible, satisfying, and measurably nothing. The binding constraint has moved to getting the page in front of people who are already looking for it, which is a harder and less pleasant problem, and the reason so many pages get optimised for the eleventh time instead.

Common questions

What is a good landing page conversion rate?
It varies enormously by traffic source, price and category. Cold visitors to a paid digital product commonly convert in the low single digits, while warm traffic from a trusted referral can run far higher. Comparing your rate to a published average is mostly noise — the useful comparison is your own page against itself over time, once you have enough visitors to measure.
How much traffic do I need before A/B testing is worth it?
Roughly a few thousand visitors per variant to detect the size of change a copy edit typically produces. Below that a test will still declare a winner, but it is reporting randomness. At low traffic, fix defects that are wrong on their own terms instead.
Should the price be on the landing page?
For low-priced digital products, showing it usually helps. A hidden price reads as a surprise being postponed, and visitors who leave to avoid finding out are indistinguishable from visitors who were never interested.
Is my problem conversion or traffic?
Divide expected sales by visitors. If you would expect fewer than about two sales a month at a typical rate, zero sales is consistent with a perfectly good page and the constraint is traffic. Optimising copy in that situation feels productive and changes nothing.
