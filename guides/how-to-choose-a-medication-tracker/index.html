How to choose a medication tracker: 8 things to check in any template, printable or app

Search for a medication tracker and you get hundreds of printables, spreadsheets and apps that look alike in the preview. They stop looking alike the first time a refill runs out on a Saturday or a new doctor asks what you take. Here is a short test to run on any of them, free or paid, including ours, before you type in a single name.

First, decide what the tracker is for

People search for a medication tracker for three quite different jobs, and the right tool depends on which one is yours.

  • Remembering to take a dose. This is a reminder job. A phone alarm, a pill organiser with a timer, or an app that notifies you does it far better than any spreadsheet or printable can.
  • Keeping an accurate list. What you take, how it is written on the label, who prescribed it, and what you react badly to. This is a records job, and it is where templates and spreadsheets are strongest.
  • Keeping someone else's list. A parent, a partner, a child. This is a records job with a second reader, which changes what matters: sharing, printing, and room for more than one person.

Most of the checks below are about the second and third jobs. If the first is all you need, an app or an alarm may be the whole answer. For what belongs on the list itself, field by field, see our companion guide How to keep a medication list.

A quick way to run this test: open the template, add one made-up prescription with a 30-day supply filled two weeks ago, one allergy, and one vitamin. Then see what the file tells you. Most of these checks answer themselves in five minutes.

The 8-point check

1. The current list is kept apart from the history

A tracker that only has one table ends up as a mix of what you take now and what you took two years ago. Look for a current list that stays short and readable, with a separate place to record what was stopped or adjusted, when, and by whom. The history is useful; it just should not be in the way.

2. Names as they appear on the label

A useful row records the name and strength exactly as the pharmacy label shows them, plus the instructions as written. Templates with a single free-text box tend to collect shorthand that nobody else can read. Separate columns for name, strength and label instructions are a good sign.

3. Allergies with the reaction, not just the name

“Allergic to penicillin” and “penicillin upset my stomach once” are very different pieces of information to the person reading your list. Check whether the template has a column for what actually happened, so the details are written down rather than remembered. Which one it counts as is for your clinician or pharmacist to judge; the tracker's only job is to record what happened and when.

4. A place for over-the-counter products and supplements

Vitamins, herbal products, pain relievers and sleep aids are the items most often missing from a list, and they are part of the picture a clinician or pharmacist wants to see. If the template only has room for prescriptions, it will quietly leave them out.

5. Refill dates that work themselves out

Running out is usually a date problem, not a memory problem. The better trackers record when a prescription was filled and how many days it lasts, and calculate the next refill date for you. A template that only has a blank “refill” column leaves the arithmetic to you at the worst moment. A calculated date is only as good as the fill date you type in, so copy it from the pharmacy label, and use the result as a prompt to call the pharmacy, not as the date a refill is allowed.

6. A one-page summary you can hand over

At an appointment, an admission or an emergency, nobody reads ten tabs. Look for a single printable page, or a wallet card, that pulls the essentials together: current list, allergies, conditions, and who to contact. Print it once to check it actually fits on a page.

7. Room for more than one person

If you keep lists for a household, check whether the tracker handles several people cleanly, with a separate list per person, rather than one long table where names get mixed up. This matters most for caregivers juggling a parent's list alongside their own. If several family members help, agree on one master copy and who updates it, so two versions of the same list never exist.

8. You know where your information lives

A printable lives in your drawer. A spreadsheet lives wherever you save it. An app may keep your list on a company's servers, sometimes with an account, analytics or sharing you did not ask for, and consumer apps are not always covered by the same privacy rules as your pharmacy or doctor. None of these is wrong. It is worth knowing which one you have chosen, and reading the privacy policy before you enter anything.

Printable, spreadsheet or app

Each format is good at something different, so the honest answer depends on the job from the first section.

  • Printable. Simplest to start and needs no device. Harder to keep current, since every adjustment means a new copy or crossings-out, and there is no arithmetic for refill dates.
  • Spreadsheet. Easy to edit, can do the refill maths, prints a clean summary, and is easy to share with a family member. It cannot remind you to take anything.
  • App. The only one of the three that can send dose reminders. The trade-offs are check 8, where your information lives, and whether the list can be printed or exported when someone asks for it.

Plenty of people use two: an alarm or app for reminders, and a list they can print for appointments. That combination covers all three jobs without asking one tool to do everything.

Free templates versus paid ones

Many free medication tracker printables and templates are perfectly good for a short list. Run the same eight checks on them as on anything you would pay for. Where free ones most often fall short is checks 1, 3 and 5: a single table with no history, an allergy column with no room for the reaction, and a refill column with no dates behind it.

If a free template passes the checks you care about, it is a fine choice. A paid one earns its price only if it saves you the work of adding what is missing.

Once you have picked one

  • Fill it in with the pharmacy bags or bottles in front of you, not from memory.
  • Bring it to your next appointment or pharmacy visit and ask them to look it over for anything missing or out of date.
  • Update it the same day anything is adjusted, and note the date and who made the adjustment.
  • Print the one-page summary and keep a copy somewhere a family member could find it.

Common questions

What should a medication tracker include?
At minimum: a current list with each name and strength as written on the label, allergies with the reaction recorded, a place for over-the-counter products and supplements, and refill dates. A one-page summary you can hand over and a separate history of past adjustments are strong additions.
Is a free printable medication tracker good enough?
Often, yes, especially for a short list. Check that it separates current items from history, records the reaction for each allergy, and has room for supplements. The main thing a printable cannot do is work out refill dates or remind you to take a dose.
Is a spreadsheet or an app better for tracking medications?
It depends on the job. An app is the only option that can send dose reminders. A spreadsheet is usually easier to edit, print and share with a family member or clinician. Many people use an alarm or app for reminders and a printable list for appointments.
